# Armenia Life Sciences Exchange — provisional business plan

**Prepared 2 October 2026.** This is a decision framework, not a verified market study. The supplied Drive folder and earlier company analysis were inaccessible from this environment, and this repository was empty. Company counts, prices, conversion rates, and revenue below are explicit working assumptions. No real provider is named in the demo. The first commercial step is to replace these assumptions with interviews, a source backed provider inventory, and a review of Armenian and destination market rules.

**Concept demo:** https://hrezaii95.github.io/armenia-CRO/

## Executive decision

Build a trusted discovery and inquiry layer for Armenian contract research and analytical services, serving Armenian and foreign life sciences buyers. Begin with a narrowly curated directory and a structured request for quotation (RFQ) flow. This solves a visible purchasing problem before attempting autonomous laboratory or clinical workflows. Add agentic assistance for provider operations only when real providers grant access to their data and a human can approve consequential outputs.

The network needs both sides, but the launch sequence should be supply first: a small group of consenting providers with complete, regularly reviewed profiles, followed by targeted buyer acquisition. Avoid paid search ranking; credibility is the primary asset. Medtech, pharmaceutical manufacturing, and cosmetics are expansion tracks with distinct buyer questions and regulatory claims. Combining all of them into a single undifferentiated index at launch would weaken search quality.

## Customer and job to be done

**Primary buyer:** a small or midsize pharma, biotech, medtech, cosmetics, or academic team that needs to identify a suitable Armenian service partner, assess fit, and send a brief that can receive a useful answer. An overseas buyer may also need English communication, export logistics, contracting, and quality documentation.

**Primary provider:** a CRO, analytical laboratory, research center, or specialized development service team that needs qualified inquiries and a way to explain capabilities without repeatedly answering basic questions. A secondary user is the provider's business development or quality lead, who must approve the public profile.

**Core problem hypothesis:** fragmented discovery and inconsistent capability descriptions increase buyer screening time and cause providers to receive poor quality leads. The solution is a comparable profile schema and an RFQ assistant that asks for the minimum useful project context.

## Product scope and positioning

1. **Find:** searchable categories for bioanalysis, preclinical research, formulation, clinical operations, testing, and scientific infrastructure. Geography is Armenia by default, but include cross border service and shipping information where confirmed.
2. **Assess:** consent based provider pages with capability evidence, modalities, quality documentation status, languages, response window, sample acceptance, and a dated source for each claim. Separate provider supplied statements from independently checked evidence.
3. **Compare:** a buyer can compare up to three providers against the same criteria. Missing data is shown as “not supplied,” never silently inferred.
4. **Contact:** a guided RFQ asks for project stage, service, material, timing, country, and confidentiality preferences. Launch with human reviewed delivery and clear consent; the demo only drafts text locally.
5. **Operate:** later, a private agent workspace can help a provider draft capability statements, triage RFQs, prepare nonbinding answers, and track handoffs. It should use approved documents, role based access, citations, audit trails, and human approval. Do not let it issue quality, safety, pricing, or regulatory commitments autonomously.

The positioning is “a trusted front door to Armenian life sciences capabilities,” rather than a social network at first. LinkedIn style identity and activity can become useful once provider identities and buyer value are established. Open social feeds at launch would add moderation cost before they improve transactions.

## Launch wedge and sequencing

| Period | Product and commercial work | Evidence to advance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weeks 1–4 | Build a consent based inventory of 20–30 potential providers; interview 10 providers and 10 buyers; choose one or two service categories | At least 8 providers confirm profile fields and 5 buyers describe a recent difficult search |
| Weeks 5–8 | Publish 8–12 reviewed profiles and a human operated RFQ concierge; instrument search to inquiry funnel | 3 completed buyer briefs and at least 2 provider responses within a mutually agreed window |
| Months 3–4 | Pilot comparison, claim review workflow, bilingual copy where evidence supports it | 10 qualified RFQs, 70% provider response within 5 business days, buyer feedback on shortlist quality |
| Months 5–6 | Test paid enhanced profile operations and buyer sourcing support; decide on adjacent verticals | 3 paying provider pilots or 2 paying buyer pilots, with retention intent and no ranking conflict |
| Months 7–12 | Add repeatable matching and a restricted provider assistant; expand category by category | Documented cost per qualified RFQ, repeat buyers, and at least one completed buyer-provider project attributable to the platform |

These are proposed gates, not forecasts. If supply is sparse or buyers will not transact through the site, switch to a specialist sourcing service or stop before building a broad platform.

## Business model and economics to test

Keep basic profiles free and review all claims under the same policy. Test an **enhanced provider workspace at $79–199/month** for profile maintenance, inquiry triage, and analytics. Test **buyer sourcing support at $300–900 per qualified search** where a human does the initial screen. These price bands are hypotheses for interviews, not validated willingness to pay. Avoid success fees until attribution, procurement rules, and conflicts are understood. Do not sell rank position.

Illustrative monthly pilot math at the midpoint: 10 provider subscribers × $129 = $1,290; 4 buyer searches × $500 = $2,000; total $3,290. If human review and outreach take 40 hours × $25 loaded cost = $1,000, infrastructure and tools $250, and acquisition $700, contribution before founders' time and general overhead is $1,340. This is a scenario to test, not a revenue prediction. Track actual staff minutes per profile, search, and RFQ; small transaction volumes can erase margin quickly.

Avoid a top down TAM claim without a verified denominator. Build a bottom up serviceable market from: eligible Armenian providers × achievable subscription adoption + reachable annual buyer searches × paid conversion × price. Validate the number of eligible providers through registries and direct outreach, and the buyer search volume through interviews and observed traffic. Armenia's domestic buyer base is likely too narrow on its own; the commercial thesis depends on cross border demand and trust.

## Go to market

Recruit three design partner providers in one service cluster, then fill adjacent capabilities with consent and a common profile schema. Reach buyers through targeted scientific communities, university and innovation networks, diaspora professional networks, industry events, and direct outreach to organizations with plausible Armenia sourcing needs. Publish useful comparison guides and sourcing checklists, each checked by a subject expert. Use inbound SEO only after profiles contain real, permissioned, dated information. Do not scrape personal profiles or publish private specifications without permission.

The first marketplace operations team needs a commercial lead who interviews buyers, a domain reviewer for claims, and an editor/operations owner. Engineering can stay lean until the concierge funnel demonstrates repeat demand. Secure introductions to local labs and regulatory experts matter more than broad paid advertising in the pilot.

## Trust, legal, and AI controls

Get company consent before a profile is presented as claimed. Store claim text, claimant, source, reviewer, and review date. Show “not supplied” for absent evidence and a correction/contact path. Verify which Armenian and destination country rules apply to advertising clinical, diagnostic, manufacturing, and cosmetic services before publishing claims. Contracting, sample transfer, personal data, clinical data, export, and IP terms require counsel and provider specific review.

RFQs may contain confidential research. The production version needs explicit buyer consent, controlled retention, access logs, clear recipient selection, and a deletion route. The AI assistant should initially work on public or provider approved materials; no protected patient or unpublished project data by default. Every generated claim, quote, scientific interpretation, or regulatory statement needs human approval and source context. Evaluate hallucinations, leakage, prompt injection, and incorrect routing before rollout.

## Measurement and kill criteria

Measure profile completeness, verified claim coverage, search-to-shortlist, shortlist-to-RFQ, RFQ-to-provider-response, time to first useful response, buyer reported fit, repeat use, and paid conversion. Count RFQs only when a buyer submits a concrete need and consents to contact; raw clicks are not demand. A useful initial target is 70% response within five business days, but set the service promise with partners.

Pause expansion if fewer than 5 of 10 interviewed buyers would use a curated Armenia shortlist, fewer than 8 providers will maintain accurate profiles, or fewer than 2 of the first 10 qualified RFQs receive a useful response. Reframe the business as bespoke sourcing if high touch service is valued but the directory does not drive discovery.

Interview questions to resolve the market fit: What was the last Armenian or regional vendor search? Which criteria excluded providers? Who approves a shortlist? What evidence is required before an RFQ? How many similar searches occur annually? What is the cost of a failed introduction? What profile information can a provider publicly substantiate? Who owns the response SLA? What would each side pay for, and what would make a listing untrustworthy?
