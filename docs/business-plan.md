## Executive decision

Build two connected products: a multilingual, source-backed discovery network for Armenian life sciences providers and **private AI agent workspaces for those providers**. Sell a scoped diagnostic, custom agent pilot, production deployment, and recurring managed support. The agent business is the **primary revenue engine**. The directory gives buyers a trusted way to find and compare providers and gives the agent business a route to customers; free basic listings and neutral ranking protect its credibility.

Begin with one or two provider service clusters and a repeated workflow: capability Q&A, RFQ intake, proposal drafting, or approved document search. Turn successful custom modules into a configurable product instead of accepting unlimited bespoke work. Medtech, pharmaceutical manufacturing, cosmetics, and research share discovery infrastructure but need distinct capability fields and claim review. The network needs both sides, but the launch sequence should be supply first: consenting providers with accurate profiles and an AI use case, followed by targeted buyer acquisition. Never sell search rank.

## Customer and job to be done

**Primary buyer:** a small or midsize pharma, biotech, medtech, cosmetics, or academic team that needs to identify a suitable Armenian service partner, assess fit, and send a brief that can receive a useful answer. An overseas buyer may also need English communication, export logistics, contracting, and quality documentation.

**Primary paying customer:** a CRO, analytical laboratory, research center, manufacturer, or specialized development team whose staff repeatedly find scattered facts, answer similar buyer questions, assemble proposals, and hand inquiries between people. The owner or commercial lead may fund a pilot; quality and IT must approve access and use. The provider also needs qualified inquiries and an accurate public profile.

**Core hypotheses to validate:** fragmented discovery and inconsistent capability descriptions increase buyer screening time; provider knowledge work is sufficiently repetitive and documented for an assistant to improve response speed and consistency; providers will pay for that improvement; and cross-border buyer demand can complement Armenia's domestic market. Interviews and observed workflows must establish these points before forecasting demand.

## Product scope and positioning

1. **Find:** searchable categories for bioanalysis, preclinical research, formulation, clinical operations, testing, and scientific infrastructure. Geography is Armenia by default, but include cross border service and shipping information where confirmed.
2. **Assess:** consent based provider pages with capability evidence, modalities, quality documentation status, languages, response window, sample acceptance, and a dated source for each claim. Separate provider supplied statements from independently checked evidence.
3. **Compare:** a buyer can compare up to three providers against the same criteria. Missing data is shown as “not supplied,” never silently inferred.
4. **Contact:** a guided RFQ asks for project stage, service, material, timing, country, and confidentiality preferences. Launch with human reviewed delivery and clear consent; the demo only drafts text locally.
5. **Operate:** a private provider workspace answers capability questions from approved materials with citations; asks for missing RFQ details; drafts nonbinding replies, proposal outlines, and multilingual handoffs; and routes each output to a named human reviewer. The workspace records document version, source, reviewer, and approval. It never sends a price, scientific conclusion, quality statement, or regulatory commitment autonomously.
6. **Customize:** sell scoped agent development around the provider's workflow. Later modules may search controlled SOPs, summarize approved project status, or prepare task handoffs. Integrations with CRM, document repositories, LIMS, ELN, or QMS require individual authorization and acceptance tests. The assistant does not replace a regulated system of record or responsible professional.

The positioning is “a trusted front door to Armenian life sciences capabilities, paired with an AI operations layer for providers.” LinkedIn-style identity and activity can become useful once provider identities and buyer value are established. Open social feeds at launch would add moderation cost before they improve transactions.

## Launch wedge and sequencing

| Period | Product and commercial work | Evidence to advance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weeks 1–4 | Build a consent-based provider inventory; interview 10 providers and 10 buyers; observe at least three real inquiry or document-search workflows; select one cluster | Three providers identify a repeated costly workflow and will evaluate a scoped assistant using approved data |
| Weeks 5–8 | Publish 8–12 reviewed profiles; operate RFQ matching manually; sell two paid workflow diagnostics and scope an agent pilot | Three concrete buyer briefs and one provider with a pilot owner, approved data, success measure, and budget |
| Months 3–4 | Deliver one or two paid capability/RFQ agent pilots with citations, a human review queue, and a locked evaluation set | Measured reduction in response time or staff effort without unacceptable errors; one client considers production terms |
| Months 5–6 | Deploy a managed workspace and one narrow integration; refine onboarding, access controls, multilingual review, and change control | Two paying agent clients or one production deployment, with delivery hours within estimate and renewal intent |
| Months 7–12 | Reuse the proven module across providers; expand directory and second workflow only where evidence supports them | Repeatable deployment, positive delivery margin, retained workspaces, and useful buyer-provider introductions |

These are proposed gates, not forecasts. If directory demand exists but providers will not buy agent work, test paid sourcing. If agent work sells but the directory produces few buyers, keep the agent practice and treat the directory as a modest credibility channel. Do not fund a social feed or autonomous regulated workflow before either core motion works.

## Business model and economics to test

**Custom agent development and recurring managed workspaces are the main income plan.** Use the following USD bands as quoting experiments, not validated Armenian prices. Quote in the customer's agreed currency after measuring workload, value, security requirements, and procurement constraints.

| Offer | Price hypothesis | Included scope |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Workflow diagnostic | $500–1,500 fixed | Document map, measured baseline workflow, access/risk review, and written pilot scope; optionally credit the fee toward a signed pilot |
| Scoped agent pilot | $8,000–20,000 fixed | One workflow, approved knowledge set, citations, human review queue, evaluation set, staff training, and measured result over 6–8 weeks |
| Production deployment and custom integrations | $20,000–60,000+ per project | Access controls, selected integration, acceptance tests, documentation, and change control |
| Managed agent workspace | $750–2,500/month per organization, with usage and support limits | Hosting, model usage allowance, monitoring, approved knowledge updates, support, and audit exports |
| Basic public profile | Free | Source-backed presence and standard inquiry route; no paid search placement |
| Buyer sourcing concierge, optional | $300–900 per qualified search | Human-reviewed shortlist where a buyer values it; secondary revenue only |

Charge for a defined workflow and ongoing operation, not “unlimited AI.” Separate implementation, recurring support, and model usage in contracts; specify data ownership, service limits, acceptance criteria, and change-order terms. A paid profile tier, if introduced, can cover editing and analytics but must not change ranking or evidence standards.

**Illustrative first-year sales scenario, not a forecast:** four paid pilots at $12,000 ($48,000), two of those clients expanding to $35,000 production deployments ($70,000), and four managed workspaces active for an average of six months at $1,000/month ($24,000) produce **$142,000 revenue**. If pilots require 120 delivery hours each, deployments 300 hours each, and workspaces eight support hours per month, total direct labor is 1,272 hours. At an assumed loaded $60/hour plus $4,320 in cloud/model costs and $20,000 in sales, travel, and legal allocation, this leaves about **$41,000 before general overhead and unallocated founder time**. At $90/hour, it falls to about **$3,000**. These are sensitivities, not achieved margins. Track actual hours, model usage, revisions, and sales cycle on every pilot; fixed-fee customization can erase margin.

Avoid a top-down TAM claim without a verified denominator. Build a bottom-up serviceable market from reachable providers × share with a recurring knowledge or inquiry problem × pilot conversion × production conversion × contract value, plus independently measured foreign demand. Validate the provider count through registries and outreach, and buyer search volume through interviews and observed traffic. Directory traffic is an acquisition indicator; signed agent contracts, renewal, and delivery margin determine whether the business works.

## Go to market

Recruit three design partners in one service cluster through direct introductions, industry groups, university and innovation networks, and relevant diaspora contacts. Ask each to nominate a workflow owner, a document owner, and a decision-maker. Sell the diagnostic around measured time lost, response delays, or inconsistent answers, then offer a fixed pilot scope. Offer wider integrations only after a successful pilot. Fill adjacent directory capabilities with consent and a common profile schema. Reach buyers through targeted scientific communities, industry events, and direct outreach; publish useful comparison guides checked by a subject expert. Use inbound SEO only after profiles contain real, permissioned, dated information. Do not scrape personal profiles or publish private specifications without permission.

The first team needs a commercial lead, a technical lead experienced in secure integrations, and part-time life sciences and quality review. Use legal and regulatory specialists for contract and workflow review. The custom work should produce reusable ingestion, retrieval, review, evaluation, access-control, and multilingual modules. Track the share of each project using the common platform; falling reuse means the company is becoming an unscalable agency. Plan cash against actual hiring and hosting quotes. Deposits and milestone billing limit working-capital exposure; do not assume venture funding to make the first contracts viable.

## Trust, legal, and AI controls

Get company consent before a profile is presented as claimed. Store claim text, claimant, source, reviewer, and review date. Show “not supplied” for absent evidence and a correction/contact path. Verify which Armenian and destination country rules apply to advertising clinical, diagnostic, manufacturing, and cosmetic services before publishing claims. Contracting, sample transfer, personal data, clinical data, export, and IP terms require counsel and provider specific review.

RFQs may contain confidential research. The production version needs explicit buyer consent, controlled retention, access logs, clear recipient selection, and a deletion route. For private workspaces, the provider controls which documents enter the knowledge base and who can read them. Use role-based access, tenant separation, encryption, audit logs, retention limits, and written terms for model providers and subprocessors. No patient, clinical, confidential buyer, or unpublished R&D data enters a pilot by default; get authorization and a reviewed data-processing arrangement before using sensitive material. Every generated claim, quote, scientific interpretation, quality statement, or regulatory statement needs human approval and source context. Test against a provider-approved question set before launch and after document/model changes; measure unsupported claims, wrong citations, leakage, prompt injection, and failed escalation. Validate any workflow that could affect regulated records with the provider and appropriate counsel.

## Measurement and kill criteria

For the **agent business**, measure paid diagnostics, pilot close rate, pilot-to-production conversion, deployment time, delivery hours against estimate, model cost per client, cited-answer accuracy on an agreed evaluation set, staff time saved, reviewer rejection rate, active users, renewals, and revenue by implementation versus recurring work. Generic “tasks automated” counts are not proof of value. For the **network**, measure approved profiles, verified claim coverage, qualified briefs, shortlist usefulness, provider response time, and introductions that lead to real discussions. Count an RFQ only when a buyer submits a concrete need and consents to contact; raw clicks are not demand.

Pause agent expansion if no provider funds a scoped pilot after ten qualified workflow interviews, if deployment cost repeatedly exceeds the fixed fee, if an agreed evaluation set reveals material unsupported claims, or if production clients cannot use the assistant within their security rules. Pause network expansion if fewer than eight providers maintain accurate profiles or fewer than two of the first ten qualified RFQs receive a useful response. Reset thresholds after real baseline data arrives.
